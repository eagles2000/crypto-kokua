1Crypto in Plain English

Cryptocurrency is digital money that lives on a public, shared record called a blockchain. No bank holds it for you. Instead, whoever controls the secret key to an address controls the coins at that address.

  • Blockchain: a public ledger that records every transaction. Once a transaction is confirmed, it cannot be reversed or "charged back."
  • Coin or token: the asset itself, such as Bitcoin (BTC), Ethereum (ETH), Solana (SOL), XRP or USDC.
  • Stablecoin: a token designed to stay worth $1, such as USDC. Useful for moving dollars quickly or parking value without price swings.
  • Network: the blockchain a coin travels on. Some tokens (like USDC) exist on several networks. You must always send on the network the receiver supports.

Exchange vs. Wallet: the two tools you will use

Exchange (Centralized)

A company, like Kraken or Coinbase, that connects to your bank so you can buy, sell and cash out. The exchange holds your crypto for you, much like a brokerage holds stocks.

Requires ID verification. Easiest on-ramp and off-ramp to dollars.

Wallet (Self-Custody)

An app or device, like Exodus, Ledger or Trezor, where you alone hold the keys. No company can freeze or lose your coins, but no company can rescue you if you lose your recovery phrase.

No account or ID needed. You are your own bank.

The saying to remember"Not your keys, not your coins." Crypto on an exchange is an IOU from that company. Crypto in a wallet whose recovery phrase you hold is truly yours.

How to store your recovery phrase

  1. Write the words by hand, in order, on paper or a steel backup plate. Double-check spelling.
  2. Never photograph it, type it into a notes app, email it, or save it to the cloud.
  3. Keep it somewhere fire-safe and private, such as a home safe or safe deposit box. Some people keep two copies in two locations.
  4. Make sure one trusted person knows where it is kept (not what it says) as part of your estate plan.

4The Golden Safety Rules

Read this before you buy anything
  1. No legitimate company will ever ask for your recovery phrase. Not Exodus, Ledger, Trezor, Kraken or Coinbase. Anyone who asks is a scammer, always.
  2. Always send a small test amount first when using a new address. Once it arrives, send the rest.
  3. Check the first and last 4 to 6 characters of every pasted address. Some malware swaps copied addresses.
  4. Match the network. Sending on the wrong network can lose funds permanently.
  5. Buy hardware wallets only from the maker (ledger.com, trezor.io, tangem.com) or an official reseller. Never buy used, and never use a device that arrives with words already written down.
  6. Bookmark the real websites. Fake sites and fake "support" accounts on social media are the most common traps.
  7. Ignore anyone promising guaranteed returns, "doubling" your crypto, or urgent action. Romance and investment scams often start with a friendly stranger.
  8. Turn on app-based 2FA for every exchange and your email account.

4AResearch Before You Buy: CoinGecko, CoinMarketCap and DeFi Llama

CoinGecko ↗ CoinMarketCap ↗

Before you buy any coin, look it up. These free sites show live prices, charts and the basic facts about thousands of cryptocurrencies.

Price and coin information

  • CoinGecko: prices, charts, market size and which exchanges list each coin. The live prices on this site come from CoinGecko.
  • CoinMarketCap: similar to CoinGecko, with rankings by market size and a watchlist.
  • DeFi Llama: for DeFi apps and blockchains: how much money each one holds, yields, stablecoins and past hacks. See the next part of this section.
  • TradingView: detailed charts for crypto, stocks and metals if you want to go deeper.
  • Crypto Kōkua Markets: a quick look at crypto, stocks and metals, up or down.

Check a transaction yourself

A block explorer is a public search engine for a blockchain. Paste in an address or a transaction ID to see whether a send went through.

  • Bitcoin: mempool.space
  • Ethereum: etherscan.io
  • Solana: solscan.io
  • XRP: xrpscan.com

What to look at before you buy

  1. Market size (market cap): bigger, older coins tend to swing less than tiny new ones.
  2. Where it trades: if it isn't on Kraken, Coinbase or another major exchange, be extra careful.
  3. How long it has existed: brand-new coins are where most scams happen.
  4. Who is promoting it: strangers, influencers and "guaranteed" returns are red flags.
Watch for fake look-alikesScammers copy the names of real coins. Always check a coin's official page on CoinGecko or CoinMarketCap before buying, and never buy because a stranger told you to.

DeFi Llama: the free research tool for DeFi

Open DeFi Llama ↗ Yields ↗ Hacks ↗ Stablecoins ↗ Unlocks ↗

DeFi Llama (written DefiLlama on the site) is a free website that tracks decentralized finance, or DeFi: the crypto apps that lend, borrow, swap and pay interest using code (smart contracts) instead of a bank or company. CoinGecko and CoinMarketCap tell you what a coin costs. DeFi Llama tells you what is happening inside crypto: where the money is, how much each app earns, which ones have been hacked, and what's coming next.

It launched in 2020, was started by a developer who goes by the name 0xngmi, and is open source, meaning anyone can check how the numbers are counted. It now follows thousands of DeFi apps on more than 500 blockchains. You don't need an account, there are no ads, and it never asks you to connect a wallet just to look.

The big number: TVL

TVL means total value locked: the dollar value of all the crypto people have deposited into an app or a blockchain. Think of it as the money in the bank's vault. A lending app with $20 billion in TVL is trusted by far more people than one with $2 million. It's not a guarantee of safety, but a big, steady TVL over several years is a good sign, and a TVL that drops fast is a warning.

What you can look up

  • TVL by app: the home page ranks DeFi apps by money deposited. Click any app, such as Aave or Kamino, to see its history, which blockchains it runs on, and whether its code has been audited.
  • Chains: compares blockchains like Ethereum, Solana, Bitcoin, XRP Ledger and Flare by TVL, fees and activity.
  • Yields: lists thousands of pools that pay interest, sorted by APY (yearly return), with filters for blockchain, coin and stablecoins only.
  • Stablecoins: shows every major stablecoin (USDC, USDT and others), how many exist, which blockchains they're on, and whether each one is holding its $1 value.
  • Fees and revenue: how much each app and blockchain actually earns. Real earnings are a sign people really use it.
  • Volume: how much is traded on decentralized exchanges and other markets each day.
  • Hacks: a public record of every major DeFi hack and theft, with the date, the amount lost and how it happened.
  • Unlocks: a calendar of when new tokens are released to a project's team and early investors. A big unlock can push a price down as people sell.
  • Bridges: how much crypto moves between blockchains.
  • Raises and treasuries: which projects raised money from investors, and how much each project holds in reserve.
  • Also tracked: real-world assets such as tokenized Treasury bills, and crypto ETFs.

How a beginner can use it

  1. Before you use a lending or earning app: look it up. Check its TVL, how long it has existed and whether it is audited. This is a good step before trying any of the borrowing apps in Section 11.
  2. Search the Hacks page: type in the app's name. If it has been hacked before, read what happened and whether users were paid back.
  3. Check a stablecoin before you hold a lot: the Stablecoins page shows if one has slipped below $1.
  4. Compare yields honestly: on the Yields page, look at base APY (interest paid from real activity) versus reward APY (paid in the app's own token, which can lose value). Very high numbers almost always mean very high risk.
  5. Check unlocks before buying a newer coin: if a large unlock is coming soon, the price may face selling pressure.

Free vs. Pro

Everything above is free. DeFi Llama also sells a Pro plan (listed at $490 a year at the time of writing) with custom dashboards, downloads and its built-in AI helper, LlamaAI, plus a separate plan for developers who need heavy data access. Most people never need to pay.

LlamaSwap

DeFi Llama also runs LlamaSwap, which checks several swap services at once to find the best price when trading coins from your own wallet, such as MetaMask or Phantom. It is an actual trading tool, not just information, so only use it once you're comfortable with self-custody wallets and network fees. Make sure you're on the real site.

How it fits with other sitesUse CoinGecko or CoinMarketCap to look up a coin's price and where it trades. Use DeFi Llama to look up an app or blockchain: how much money it holds, what it earns and whether it has been hacked. Together they give you the full picture.
Information, not adviceDeFi Llama shows data, not recommendations. A high yield or fast-growing TVL is not a reason to invest. Looking at DeFi Llama never requires connecting a wallet, so be suspicious of any copycat site or ad that asks you to connect or sign something to "see the data". Type defillama.com yourself.

5Your Getting-Started Roadmap

  1. Pick one main exchange. Kraken is a strong first choice: low fees on Kraken Pro, a long security record, and a good debit card. (See Section 6.)
  2. Verify your identity. Have your driver's license or passport and your Social Security number ready. Approval is often same-day.
  3. Secure the account. Turn on an authenticator app for 2FA and use a unique, strong password.
  4. Link your bank and make a small first deposit. (Walkthrough 8A.)
  5. Buy a small amount of a major coin to learn the process. Once you are comfortable, learn the limit order (Walkthrough 8F). It is the cheapest way to buy and sell.
  6. Install a self-custody wallet such as Exodus, write down the 12 words, and practice sending a small amount from the exchange to the wallet and back.
  7. As your holdings grow, add a Ledger, Trezor or Tangem for long-term storage.
  8. Keep records from day one (dates, amounts, prices). It makes tax time painless. (Section 10.)

8Step-by-Step Walkthroughs

8A. Link your bank and buy crypto (Kraken example)

  1. Open Kraken and tap Deposit (or Transfer > Deposit), then choose USD.
  2. Choose ACH (free) and connect your bank through the secure login screen (Plaid). Some banks use small test deposits instead.
  3. Enter an amount and confirm. Your bank usually releases funds in 1 to 5 business days. New accounts may have a short hold before you can withdraw crypto bought with ACH funds.
  4. When the dollars appear, open Kraken Pro, search the coin (for example, BTC/USD), choose Buy, enter an amount and confirm. For the lowest fee, use a limit order (Walkthrough 8F).
  5. Optional: set up recurring buys (for example, $50 every week). Buying a fixed amount on a schedule, called dollar-cost averaging, takes the guesswork out of timing.

The same flow works on Coinbase (Settings > Payment methods), Uphold (Transact > From: Bank) and Crypto.com (Transfer > Deposit > Fiat).

8B. Send crypto from Exodus to Kraken

  1. In Kraken: tap Deposit, pick the coin (for example, Bitcoin), and choose the network. Copy the deposit address. If Kraken shows a memo or tag, copy that too.
  2. In Exodus: tap the same coin, then Send. Paste the Kraken address into the address field (and memo, if required).
  3. Check: the coin and the network match on both sides, and the first and last characters of the address match exactly.
  4. Send a small test first (for example, $20 worth). Review the network fee and confirm.
  5. Wait for confirmations. Bitcoin can take 10 to 60 minutes; many other networks take seconds to minutes. Kraken shows the deposit as pending, then credited.
  6. Once the test arrives, send the remainder the same way.
Good to knowMoving your own crypto between your own wallets and accounts is not a taxable event. Keep a record of what you originally paid so you can report your cost basis when you eventually sell. Exchanges only know the cost of coins you bought on their platform.

8C. Helping a beginner receive crypto from you

If you are giving crypto to someone who has never used it, the safest approach is for them to set up their own wallet or account and give you only their public receive address.

  1. They choose where to receive it. Exodus is easiest for holding; Kraken or Coinbase is easiest if they may want to cash out to their bank.
  2. They back up their recovery phrase (Exodus) or turn on 2FA (exchange) before anything is sent. Sit with them while they do this if you can.
  3. They tap the coin, then Receive, and send you the address by text or email. For an exchange, they also confirm the network and any memo or tag.
  4. You send a small test from your Exodus. They confirm it arrived.
  5. You send the rest. Save the transaction ID (Exodus shows it in the activity list) as your record.
  6. Give them this guide and the Golden Safety Rules. New crypto owners are prime scam targets.
Avoid "handing over" a walletGiving someone your recovery phrase so they can "use your wallet" means you both control it forever. Either of you could empty it, and any device that held the words is a risk. Sending to a fresh wallet they create is cleaner and safer.
Gift tax basicsA gift is not income to the person receiving it. In 2026 you can give up to $19,000 per person per year without filing anything. Above that, the giver files IRS Form 709, but tax is rarely owed because of the large lifetime exemption. The recipient generally takes over your original cost basis, so tell them what you paid and when.

8D. Move savings from an exchange to a hardware wallet

  2. In the companion app, choose Receive. Confirm the address on the device screen, not just the computer.
  3. In your exchange, choose Withdraw or Send, paste the address, select the matching network, and send a test amount.
  4. After the test arrives, send the rest. Exchanges may charge a small withdrawal fee.

8F. Place a limit order (Kraken Pro and Crypto.com Exchange)

A limit order lets you choose the exact price you will pay or accept. A buy limit fills only at your price or lower. A sell limit fills only at your price or higher. If the market never reaches your price, nothing happens; the order simply waits until you cancel it.

Why it saves money: maker vs. taker

  • Maker: your order waits on the order book. This is the cheaper fee.
  • Taker: your order fills instantly against someone else's. This is the more expensive fee. Market orders are always taker.

The golden rule for the maker feeWhen buying, set your price a little below the current price. When selling, set it a little above. Then turn on Post Only, which means "only place this order if it will wait on the book." If your price would fill instantly at the taker fee, the exchange cancels it instead of charging you more.
Common mistakeSetting a buy price above the current market price (or a sell price below it) makes the order fill immediately at the taker fee. Post Only protects you from this.

Reading the order book

  • The order book has two sides: Bids (buyers, usually green) and Asks (sellers, usually red).
  • The gap between the highest bid and the lowest ask is the spread.
  • For a maker buy, use a price at or below the highest bid. For a maker sell, use a price at or above the lowest ask.

On Kraken Pro (pro.kraken.com)

  2. Open the order form on the right side of the screen and click Buy.
  3. Choose the order type: select Limit (not Market).
  4. Enter your Limit price. Check the order book and enter a price at or slightly below the highest bid.
  5. Enter how much: type the coin amount in Quantity, or the dollars you want to spend in Total. The two boxes adjust to match each other.
  6. Set the options: check Post only and leave Time in force on GTC (good until canceled).
  7. Review the estimated total and fee at the bottom of the form.
  8. Submit: click the green Buy button, read the confirmation, and confirm.
  9. Track it under Open orders in the panel at the bottom of the screen. You can edit the price or cancel from there at no cost. Filled orders move to your history and the coins appear in your balances.

  4. Enter your Price at or slightly below the highest bid.

  1. On your phone, open the App Store or Google Play and install Google Authenticator (publisher: Google LLC).

10Taxes: What You Owe and When

The IRS treats crypto as property, like stock. You owe tax when you dispose of it at a gain, not when it goes up in value.

Taxable events

  • Selling crypto for dollars
  • Trading one coin for another (including swaps in Exodus)
  • Spending crypto with a card or on purchases
  • Earning crypto (staking rewards, mining, airdrops, pay): taxed as income when received
  • A loan being liquidated (counts as a sale)

Not taxable

  • Buying crypto with dollars
  • Holding, no matter how much it rises
  • Moving crypto between your own wallets and accounts
  • Giving a gift (giver may file Form 709 if over $19,000)
  • Borrowing against your crypto
  • Any trading inside a Roth IRA

Short-term vs. long-term

  • Held one year or less: short-term gain, taxed at your ordinary income rate (10% to 37% federal).
  • Held more than one year: long-term gain, taxed at the lower 0%, 15% or 20% federal rates. Simply waiting past the one-year mark can cut your tax bill dramatically.

11Borrowing Against Your Crypto

Instead of selling (and paying tax), you can pledge crypto as collateral and borrow against it. You keep the coins and any future price gains, and borrowing itself is not a taxable event.

How it works

  1. You lock crypto (for example, Bitcoin) as collateral.
  2. You borrow a percentage of its value, called the loan-to-value (LTV). Borrow $25,000 against $100,000 of Bitcoin and your LTV is 25%.
  3. Interest accrues on the loan. Some platforms require no monthly payments; you repay when you choose.
  4. If the price of your collateral falls and your LTV rises past the platform's limit, your collateral is automatically sold (liquidated) to repay the loan. A liquidation counts as a sale for tax purposes, and you lose upside on those coins.

Where U.S. customers can borrow

  • Coinbase: the most accessible option. Borrow USDC against Bitcoin (up to $5 million) and against Ethereum (up to $1 million), plus XRP, Dogecoin, Cardano and Litecoin (up to $100,000). Variable and fixed rates, advertised starting around 5% to 6%. For Bitcoin and Ethereum, liquidation occurs if LTV reaches 86%. Not available in New York.
  • Kraken Flexline: fixed-rate loans of 10% to 25% APR for terms of two days to two years, limited to high-net-worth "Eligible Contract Participants."
  • Uphold Instant Credit: borrow against Bitcoin, Ethereum or XRP through the Exactly Protocol, at fixed rates from 4.28% APR, with no credit check and no early repayment penalty. Select U.S. states.
  • Specialist lenders: companies like Ledn offer fixed-rate Bitcoin-backed loans in dollars, typically around 9% to 12% APR at up to 50% LTV.
Borrow conservativelyCrypto can fall 50% or more in a bad year. Keeping your LTV at 25% or lower gives you a large cushion. At 25% LTV on Coinbase, your collateral would have to fall about 70% before liquidation. Set price alerts and be ready to add collateral or repay if prices drop.

Borrowing on other coins (no swap to Bitcoin needed)

If you hold XRP, Ethereum, Solana or another coin, you can often borrow against it directly. Rates and rules change often, so check the lender's website before applying.

13APrediction Markets: Kalshi and Polymarket

Kalshi ↗ Polymarket ↗

A prediction market lets you buy a contract on whether something will happen: will the Fed cut rates, will Bitcoin close above a price, who wins a game or an election. Each contract pays $1 if you're right and $0 if you're wrong.

How the price works

The price is the crowd's guess at the odds. A "Yes" contract at 30¢ means traders think there's about a 30% chance. Buy 100 Yes contracts at 30¢ for $30. If it happens you collect $100; if not, you lose the $30. You can also sell before the event if the price moves your way.

AirdropFree tokens sent to wallets, usually to promote a project. Taxable as income when received.
Cold storageKeeping keys offline, usually on a hardware wallet.
Cost basisWhat you paid for a coin, including fees. Used to calculate your gain or loss.
CustodialA company holds your crypto for you (exchanges).
Dollar-cost averagingBuying a fixed dollar amount on a regular schedule regardless of price.
FXRPA token on the Flare blockchain that tracks XRP one-to-one, so XRP can be used in lending apps.
Gas / network feeThe fee paid to the blockchain to process a transaction.
Health factorOn DeFi lending apps like Aave, a safety score for a loan. Above 1 is safe; at 1 or below, collateral is sold.
KYC"Know Your Customer": the ID check exchanges are required to perform.
Layer 2A faster, cheaper network that runs on top of a main blockchain such as Ethereum and settles back to it. Arbitrum and Robinhood Chain are examples.
Limit orderAn order to buy at or below, or sell at or above, a price you choose. It waits until the market reaches your price.
LiquidationAutomatic sale of loan collateral when its value falls too far.
LTVLoan-to-value: the loan amount divided by the collateral's value.
Maker / takerA maker order waits on the order book and pays the lower fee. A taker order fills instantly and pays the higher fee.
Market orderAn order that buys or sells immediately at the best available price. Always charged the taker fee.
Multisig (2-of-3)A wallet with three keys where any two are needed to send. Losing one key does not lose the coins.
NFCThe short-range wireless tap a Tangem card uses to talk to your phone.
Non-custodial / self-custodyYou hold your own keys (Exodus, Ledger, Trezor).
Order bookThe live list of buy orders (bids) and sell orders (asks) waiting on an exchange.
Post OnlyAn order setting that guarantees the maker fee by canceling the order if it would fill instantly.
Qualified custodianA regulated bank or trust company with a legal duty to protect client assets, such as BitGo or Coinbase Custody.
SpreadThe hidden cost built into a buy or sell price.
StablecoinA token designed to hold a steady $1 value, such as USDC.
StakingLocking certain coins to help secure a network in exchange for rewards.
SwapTrading one coin directly for another. A taxable event in a regular account.
Tokenized stockA token on a blockchain that represents a share of a real stock, such as Robinhood's Stock Tokens.
Transaction ID (TXID)A unique receipt number for a blockchain transaction, searchable on a block explorer.
